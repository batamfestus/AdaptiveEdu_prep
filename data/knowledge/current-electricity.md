topic: Current Electricity

## Apply Ohm's law
For an ohmic conductor at constant temperature $V = IR$, with voltage in volts, current in amperes and resistance in ohms. The resistance of a wire is $R = \rho L / A$.

Worked example: 12 V across a 4 $\Omega$ resistor gives $I = 12/4 = 3\ A$.

Common mistakes: applying $V = IR$ with a constant $R$ to non-ohmic parts such as a filament lamp or a diode, and mixing up voltage and current.

## Analyse series and parallel circuits
Series: the same current flows through every component, the voltages add up, and $R = R_1 + R_2 + \dots$. Parallel: every branch has the same voltage, the currents add up, and $1/R = 1/R_1 + 1/R_2 + \dots$. For two resistors in parallel $R = R_1R_2/(R_1 + R_2)$, and the total is always smaller than the smallest branch. A cell with internal resistance $r$ gives $E = V + Ir = I(R + r)$.

Worked example: 6 $\Omega$ and 3 $\Omega$ in parallel give $18/9 = 2\ \Omega$. Across 12 V the total current is $12/2 = 6\ A$.

Common mistakes: adding resistors directly when they are in parallel, and thinking current is "used up" as it passes round a series circuit.

## Calculate electrical energy and power
Electrical power $P = IV = I^2R = V^2/R$ (watts). Electrical energy $W = Pt = IVt$ (joules). One kilowatt-hour is $3.6 \times 10^6\ J$. Cost = number of kWh $\times$ price per kWh. A fuse is rated just above the normal current $I = P/V$.

Worked example: a 60 W lamp used for 5 h uses $0.06 \times 5 = 0.3\ kWh$.

Common mistakes: not converting hours or minutes to seconds when working in joules, and confusing kWh (energy) with kW (power).
